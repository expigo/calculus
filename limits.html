Limits and Continuity

Introduction

Limits are the foundation of calculus. They allow us to rigorously define derivatives, integrals, and understand the behavior of functions. In machine learning, limits help us understand convergence of optimization algorithms and the behavior of functions at critical points.

Why This Matters for ML:
  • Understanding when gradient descent converges
  • Analyzing the behavior of loss functions
  • Studying activation functions at boundaries
  • Continuous functions are essential for optimization theory

Intuitive Understanding of Limits

Informally, \( \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L \) means that as \( x \) gets arbitrarily close to \( a \) (but not equal to \( a \)), \( f(x) \) gets arbitrarily close to \( L \).

Example: Consider \( \lim_{x \to 2} (3x + 1) \).

As \( x \) approaches 2, we can create a table of values:

  • When \( x = 1.9 \): \( f(1.9) = 6.7 \)
  • When \( x = 1.99 \): \( f(1.99) = 6.97 \)
  • When \( x = 1.999 \): \( f(1.999) = 6.997 \)
  • When \( x = 2.001 \): \( f(2.001) = 7.003 \)
  • When \( x = 2.01 \): \( f(2.01) = 7.03 \)
  • When \( x = 2.1 \): \( f(2.1) = 7.3 \)

We see that as \( x \to 2 \), \( f(x) \to 7 \). Therefore, \( \lim_{x \to 2} (3x + 1) = 7 \).

Formal Definition (ε-δ Definition)

Definition: Limit of a Function

Let \( f \) be a function defined on an open interval containing \( a \) (except possibly at \( a \) itself). We say that

\[ \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L \]

if for every \( \varepsilon > 0 \), there exists a \( \delta > 0 \) such that

\[ 0 < |x - a| < \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon \]

What does this mean?

The ε-δ definition formalizes the intuitive idea: no matter how small a tolerance ε we choose around L, we can find a neighborhood around a (of size δ) such that all function values in that neighborhood are within our tolerance of L.

  • ε (epsilon): How close we want f(x) to be to L
  • δ (delta): How close x needs to be to a to achieve that

Limit Laws

If \( \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L \) and \( \lim_{x \to a} g(x) = M \), then:

Theorem: Limit Laws

  1. Sum Rule: \( \lim_{x \to a} [f(x) + g(x)] = L + M \)
  2. Difference Rule: \( \lim_{x \to a} [f(x) - g(x)] = L - M \)
  3. Constant Multiple Rule: \( \lim_{x \to a} [c \cdot f(x)] = c \cdot L \)
  4. Product Rule: \( \lim_{x \to a} [f(x) \cdot g(x)] = L \cdot M \)
  5. Quotient Rule: \( \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{L}{M} \) (if \( M \neq 0 \))
  6. Power Rule: \( \lim_{x \to a} [f(x)]^n = L^n \)

Example: Evaluate \( \lim_{x \to 3} (2x^2 + 5x - 7) \)

Using limit laws:

\[ \begin{align*} \lim_{x \to 3} (2x^2 + 5x - 7) &= \lim_{x \to 3} 2x^2 + \lim_{x \to 3} 5x - \lim_{x \to 3} 7 \\ &= 2 \lim_{x \to 3} x^2 + 5 \lim_{x \to 3} x - 7 \\ &= 2(3)^2 + 5(3) - 7 \\ &= 18 + 15 - 7 = 26 \end{align*} \]

One-Sided Limits

Sometimes we need to consider limits from only one direction:

  • Right-hand limit: \( \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) \) (approaching from the right)
  • Left-hand limit: \( \lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) \) (approaching from the left)

Theorem: Existence of Limits

\( \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L \) if and only if \( \lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = L \)

Example: ReLU Function

The ReLU (Rectified Linear Unit) activation function is defined as:

\[ \text{ReLU}(x) = \max(0, x) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } x < 0 \\ x & \text{if } x \geq 0 \end{cases} \]

At \( x = 0 \):

  • \( \lim_{x \to 0^-} \text{ReLU}(x) = 0 \)
  • \( \lim_{x \to 0^+} \text{ReLU}(x) = 0 \)
  • \( \lim_{x \to 0} \text{ReLU}(x) = 0 \)

The limit exists and equals 0, even though the derivative doesn't exist at this point!

Continuity

Definition: Continuity at a Point

A function \( f \) is continuous at \( x = a \) if:

  1. \( f(a) \) is defined
  2. \( \lim_{x \to a} f(x) \) exists
  3. \( \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a) \)

Types of Discontinuities:

  • Removable: Can be "fixed" by redefining the function at a point
  • Jump: Left and right limits exist but are different
  • Infinite: Function approaches infinity

Intermediate Value Theorem

If \( f \) is continuous on \( [a, b] \) and \( N \) is any number between \( f(a) \) and \( f(b) \), then there exists a number \( c \in (a, b) \) such that \( f(c) = N \).

ML Implication: Continuity is crucial for gradient descent. If the loss function is continuous, we're guaranteed that small changes in parameters lead to small changes in loss, making optimization feasible.

L'Hôpital's Rule

L'Hôpital's Rule

If \( \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = 0 \) and \( \lim_{x \to a} g(x) = 0 \) (or both approach \( \pm\infty \)), and if \( \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)} \) exists, then:

\[ \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)} \]

Example 1: Evaluate \( \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} \)

This is a \( \frac{0}{0} \) indeterminate form. Applying L'Hôpital's rule:

\[ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\cos x}{1} = \frac{\cos 0}{1} = 1 \]

Example 2: Evaluate \( \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{e^x - 1}{x} \)

Again, \( \frac{0}{0} \) form:

\[ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{e^x - 1}{x} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{e^x}{1} = e^0 = 1 \]

ML Application: L'Hôpital's rule is useful when analyzing the behavior of activation functions and loss functions near critical points. For example, understanding how \( \frac{\sin(x)}{x} \to 1 \) as \( x \to 0 \) helps in understanding certain neural network initializations.

Connection to Machine Learning

1. Convergence of Gradient Descent

Gradient descent updates parameters iteratively:

\[ \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \alpha \nabla_\theta L(\theta_t) \]

We say the algorithm converges if:

\[ \lim_{t \to \infty} \theta_t = \theta^* \]

where \( \theta^* \) is the optimal parameter. Understanding limits helps us prove convergence conditions!

2. Activation Functions and Continuity

Common activation functions:

  • Sigmoid: \( \sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}} \) — Continuous everywhere
  • ReLU: \( \text{ReLU}(x) = \max(0, x) \) — Continuous everywhere (but not differentiable at 0)
  • Tanh: \( \tanh(x) = \frac{e^x - e^{-x}}{e^x + e^{-x}} \) — Continuous everywhere

3. Loss Function Behavior

Understanding limits helps analyze loss functions:

  • What happens as predictions approach true values?
  • How does the loss behave at extremes?
  • Are there discontinuities that could cause optimization issues?

Python Examples

Computing Limits Numerically

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy import optimize

# Example 1: Numerical limit computation
def compute_limit(f, a, epsilon=1e-6):
    """
    Numerically compute limit of f(x) as x approaches a.
    """
    x_left = a - epsilon
    x_right = a + epsilon

    left_limit = f(x_left)
    right_limit = f(x_right)

    if np.abs(left_limit - right_limit) < epsilon * 10:
        return (left_limit + right_limit) / 2
    else:
        return None  # Limit doesn't exist

# Test with a simple function
f = lambda x: (x**2 - 4) / (x - 2)  # Removable discontinuity at x=2
print(f"Limit as x->2: {compute_limit(f, 2)}")  # Should be 4

# Example 2: Visualizing limits
def visualize_limit(f, a, x_range=2):
    """
    Visualize function behavior near a point.
    """
    # Create points approaching from both sides
    x_left = np.linspace(a - x_range, a - 0.01, 100)
    x_right = np.linspace(a + 0.01, a + x_range, 100)

    y_left = f(x_left)
    y_right = f(x_right)

    plt.figure(figsize=(10, 6))
    plt.plot(x_left, y_left, 'b-', label='Left approach')
    plt.plot(x_right, y_right, 'r-', label='Right approach')
    plt.axvline(x=a, color='g', linestyle='--', alpha=0.5, label=f'x = {a}')

    # Try to compute limit
    limit_val = compute_limit(f, a)
    if limit_val is not None:
        plt.axhline(y=limit_val, color='purple',
                   linestyle='--', alpha=0.5,
                   label=f'Limit = {limit_val:.2f}')
        plt.plot(a, limit_val, 'ro', markersize=10)

    plt.xlabel('x')
    plt.ylabel('f(x)')
    plt.title(f'Limit Behavior as x → {a}')
    plt.legend()
    plt.grid(True, alpha=0.3)
    plt.show()

# Visualize the removable discontinuity
visualize_limit(f, 2)

Testing Continuity

def is_continuous(f, a, epsilon=1e-6, delta=1e-6):
    """
    Test if function f is continuous at point a.
    Returns True if continuous, False otherwise.
    """
    try:
        # Check if f(a) is defined
        f_a = f(a)

        # Check if limit exists and equals f(a)
        limit_val = compute_limit(f, a, epsilon)

        if limit_val is None:
            return False

        return np.abs(limit_val - f_a) < epsilon
    except:
        return False

# Test ReLU continuity
relu = lambda x: np.maximum(0, x)
print(f"ReLU continuous at 0: {is_continuous(relu, 0)}")  # True
print(f"ReLU continuous at 1: {is_continuous(relu, 1)}")  # True

# Test step function (discontinuous)
step = lambda x: 0 if x < 0 else 1
print(f"Step function continuous at 0: {is_continuous(step, 0)}")  # False

L'Hôpital's Rule Application

from scipy.misc import derivative

def lhopital(f, g, a, order=1):
    """
    Apply L'Hôpital's rule to compute limit of f(x)/g(x) as x->a.
    order: how many times to differentiate (for multiple applications)
    """
    # Compute derivatives
    for _ in range(order):
        f_prime = lambda x: derivative(f, x, dx=1e-6)
        g_prime = lambda x: derivative(g, x, dx=1e-6)
        f, g = f_prime, g_prime

    # Compute limit of ratio of derivatives
    return compute_limit(lambda x: f(x) / g(x), a)

# Example: lim (sin(x)/x) as x->0
f = lambda x: np.sin(x)
g = lambda x: x
result = lhopital(f, g, 0)
print(f"lim sin(x)/x as x->0 = {result:.6f}")  # Should be 1

# Example: lim (e^x - 1)/x as x->0
f = lambda x: np.exp(x) - 1
g = lambda x: x
result = lhopital(f, g, 0)
print(f"lim (e^x - 1)/x as x->0 = {result:.6f}")  # Should be 1

Gradient Descent Convergence Visualization

def gradient_descent_convergence():
    """
    Visualize convergence of gradient descent (limit of theta_t as t->infinity).
    """
    # Simple quadratic function: f(x) = (x - 3)^2
    f = lambda x: (x - 3)**2
    df = lambda x: 2*(x - 3)

    # Gradient descent
    learning_rate = 0.1
    x = 0.0  # Starting point
    history = [x]

    for _ in range(50):
        x = x - learning_rate * df(x)
        history.append(x)

    # Plot convergence
    plt.figure(figsize=(12, 5))

    # Plot 1: Parameter value over iterations
    plt.subplot(1, 2, 1)
    plt.plot(history, 'b-o', markersize=3)
    plt.axhline(y=3, color='r', linestyle='--',
               label='Optimal value (limit)')
    plt.xlabel('Iteration')
    plt.ylabel('Parameter value')
    plt.title('Convergence to Optimal Value')
    plt.legend()
    plt.grid(True, alpha=0.3)

    # Plot 2: Loss over iterations
    plt.subplot(1, 2, 2)
    losses = [f(x) for x in history]
    plt.semilogy(losses, 'g-o', markersize=3)
    plt.xlabel('Iteration')
    plt.ylabel('Loss (log scale)')
    plt.title('Loss Convergence')
    plt.grid(True, alpha=0.3)

    plt.tight_layout()
    plt.show()

    print(f"Final parameter value: {history[-1]:.6f}")
    print(f"Optimal value: 3.0")
    print(f"Difference: {abs(history[-1] - 3):.2e}")

Exercises

Exercise 1: Basic Limits

Evaluate the following limits:

  1. \( \lim_{x \to 3} (2x^2 - 5x + 1) \)
  2. \( \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x^2 + 3x}{x} \)
  3. \( \lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2} \)
  4. \( \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{3x^2 + 2x}{x^2 - 1} \)

Solutions:

  1. \( \lim_{x \to 3} (2x^2 - 5x + 1) = 2(9) - 5(3) + 1 = 18 - 15 + 1 = 4 \)
  2. \( \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x^2 + 3x}{x} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x(x + 3)}{x} = \lim_{x \to 0} (x + 3) = 3 \)
  3. \( \lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2} = \lim_{x \to 2} \frac{(x-2)(x+2)}{x - 2} = \lim_{x \to 2} (x + 2) = 4 \)
  4. \( \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{3x^2 + 2x}{x^2 - 1} = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{3 + \frac{2}{x}}{1 - \frac{1}{x^2}} = \frac{3}{1} = 3 \)

Exercise 2: Continuity

Determine if the following functions are continuous at the given point. If not, classify the type of discontinuity.

  1. \( f(x) = \frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1} \) at \( x = 1 \)
  2. \( g(x) = \begin{cases} x^2 & x < 2 \\ 5 & x = 2 \\ 2x & x > 2 \end{cases} \) at \( x = 2 \)
  3. \( h(x) = \frac{1}{x - 3} \) at \( x = 3 \)

Solutions:

  1. Removable discontinuity. \( \lim_{x \to 1} f(x) = 2 \) but \( f(1) \) is undefined.
  2. Jump discontinuity. \( \lim_{x \to 2^-} g(x) = 4 \) and \( \lim_{x \to 2^+} g(x) = 4 \), but \( g(2) = 5 \). Actually, this is removable!
  3. Infinite discontinuity. \( \lim_{x \to 3} h(x) = \pm\infty \)

Exercise 3: L'Hôpital's Rule

Use L'Hôpital's rule to evaluate:

  1. \( \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\tan x}{x} \)
  2. \( \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{e^{2x} - 1}{x} \)
  3. \( \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos x}{x^2} \)

Solutions:

  1. \( \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\tan x}{x} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sec^2 x}{1} = 1 \)
  2. \( \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{e^{2x} - 1}{x} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{2e^{2x}}{1} = 2 \)
  3. \( \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos x}{x^2} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{2x} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\cos x}{2} = \frac{1}{2} \) (applied twice)

Exercise 4: ML Application

The sigmoid function is \( \sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}} \).

  1. Compute \( \lim_{x \to \infty} \sigma(x) \)
  2. Compute \( \lim_{x \to -\infty} \sigma(x) \)
  3. Is \( \sigma(x) \) continuous everywhere? Prove it.
  4. Why is continuity important for using sigmoid as an activation function?

Solutions:

  1. \( \lim_{x \to \infty} \sigma(x) = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}} = \frac{1}{1 + 0} = 1 \)
  2. \( \lim_{x \to -\infty} \sigma(x) = \lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}} = \frac{1}{1 + \infty} = 0 \)
  3. Yes, it's continuous everywhere. The denominator is never zero (since \( 1 + e^{-x} > 1 \) for all \( x \)), and it's a composition of continuous functions.
  4. Continuity ensures smooth gradients for backpropagation and prevents sudden jumps in the network's output.
